Watching people get OpenClaw installed at Tencent Tower today gave me a lot to think about. It feels a bit like "The Great OpenClaw Leap Forward."

Many big tech companies are aggressively pushing non-technical frontline employees to install this AI tool, and I've even seen people online offering home installation for 500 RMB. Everyone's desperately searching for uses for it, demanding results, and trying to prove this thing is too important to miss. The whole thing feels like people living in different technological worlds, folded into the same place.

I came across an interesting quote: "If you can't even install OpenClaw, how would you use it?" Taking it a step further, if you haven't even learned the basics, being expected to build a whole workflow, deliver results, and prove its value is even harder.

There are two things overlapping here. One is illusion: many executives have watched too many short video clips, repeatedly bombarded by exaggerated narratives and "universal solution" case studies, until they start believing this tool can do everything, connect to anything, and should be installed by everyone, with results to show for it straight away. The other is anxiety: everyone fears missing this wave, so they start ordering people to adopt it, substituting collective anxiety for real demand.

So you see a strong contrast. On one side, slogans are grand, as if everyone must enter the AI-native era. On the other side, many people can't even clearly articulate what tasks are worth handing over to it. This contrast will only intensify, and become increasingly absurd.

Because tools never generate value through installation alone. A tool is useful when there's enough work for it to do, a clear process, and a way to tell whether the result is right. Without recurring tasks, SOPs, work that can be done online, and clear inputs and outputs, even the most powerful tool is just an icon. It won't automatically grow use cases just because it's installed.

So I've always felt that OpenClaw isn't suitable for everyone.

It suits people who can direct the work, and people running a one-person company. It fits people who constantly have tasks to delegate, can break work into steps, and complete many things online. Especially if you've used skills and tools, understand AI's capability boundaries, can connect the steps of a workflow and get things done, it can be a very good fit.

For me, there's a natural use for it. When I have plenty to do but I'm away from home or the office, with just my phone or no easy way to open a laptop, I put my two nanobots to work: one checks issues in my open-source projects and proposes a technical plan, while the other reviews and commits. I can get it all done on my morning commute. It's so convenient.

But if someone has no work to do while they're out, or doesn't even want to open a computer at home, how are they supposed to invent a use for it? Enjoying good food and having fun is a perfectly nice way to spend your time. If you have no use for it, you have no use for it. There's really no need to worry.

I think what's most easily amplified in this wave isn't the gap in ability, but the gap in having something to use it for. People with real work for it will get more comfortable and faster with it, until it feels like having a few extra versions of themselves. People without that work can get stuck going around in concepts, tutorials, case studies, and videos, ending up with nothing changed except having installed more software.

Many people's biggest problem today isn't that they haven't installed OpenClaw, it's that they mistake installing a tool for having entered the AI era. The real dividing line has always been understanding the task, designing the workflow, and judging the result. Do you actually have continuous problems to solve? Can you break problems down and hand them to the system? Can you judge whether results are correct? These determine whether you can truly extract value from AI.

So no need for anxiety. Installing OpenClaw when you have nothing to use it for doesn't mean much.

If you really want to experience where this generation of AI shines, better to spend $20 on Claude Code, or more interestingly, get a ChatGPT subscription and use GPT 5.4 to help you tackle something you genuinely find difficult. Have it work out a plan, carry it out, and experience what it's like to get a problem solved so directly and efficiently. That's far better than installing OpenClaw.

OpenClaw suits people with work for it to do, people who can direct that work, and people running a one-person company. It suits those who can turn a process into repeatable steps and get it done online. It's certainly powerful, but that power shows in the work it completes for you, not in being installed.

Many people today are installing OpenClaw, but what they should really figure out first is one question: what problems do I actually have that are worth solving with AI?

That question might be more important than installing anything.
